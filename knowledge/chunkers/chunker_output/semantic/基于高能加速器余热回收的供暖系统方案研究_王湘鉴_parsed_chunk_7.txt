Abstract: [Objective] Amidst China’s national strategy to achieve carbon peak and carbon neutrality, the transition towards clean, low-carbon heating systems has become a critical priority. This shift is especially urgent in northern regions where conventional fossil fuel-based heating impacts significantly to carbon emissions and air pollution. Large-scale research infrastructures, such as high-energy particle accelerators, represent concentrated energy consumption nodes, with much of their electricity input ultimately rejected as low-grade thermal energy through cooling towers. Harnessing this largely untapped waste heat for space heating presents a dual opportunity: improving the energy efficiency of major scientific facilities while displacing carbon-intensive heating sources. This study addresses the specific technical and operational challenges of recovering low-grade, intermittent waste heat from an operational accelerator, using the Beijing Electron-Positron Collider (BEPC) as a representative case to develop and comprehensively evaluate a practical heating solution. The research is motivated by the significant thermal resource available at facilities like BEPC and the pressing need to decarbonize municipal heating system. While international examples such as CERN demonstrate the principle, applying waste heat recovery to existing, medium-scale accelerators with variable operational schedules poses distinct challenges. This study explicitly targets three core problems: (1) accurately quantifying the available waste heat potential and matching its intermittent profile with stable building heating demand; (2) designing a non-intrusive system integration scheme that guarantees zero interference with the accelerator’s primary scientific operations; and (3) conducting a holistic techno-economic-environmental assessment to verify the project’s viability and provide a replicable evaluation framework. [Methods] The methodology follows a systematic, data-driven approach. Subsequently, an integrated system was designed, centering on a screw-type compression heat pump. This technology was selected after a detailed comparison with centrifugal and absorption alternatives, with decision factors emphasizing excellent part-load performance, tolerance to source temperature variation, and favorable lifecycle costs. The system architecture prioritizes accelerator operational safety, incorporates redundancy with a backup connection to the campus heating network, and was spatially planned using Building Information Modeling (BIM) for optimal layout. A comprehensive evaluation model was then constructed, integrating thermodynamic performance (Coefficient of Performance, COP), full lifecycle economic analysis (including capital investment, operational savings, and payback period), and carbon emission reduction calculations.
